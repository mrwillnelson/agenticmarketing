# Milestone Hooks

The news is not the hook. Readers scroll past a hundred announcements a week, and every one of them opens the same way. What stops them is the reality behind the milestone: the friction that existed before it, the cost somebody paid, the bet it proves. Load `hooks` first; this skill changes where the voltage comes from when the source is company news.

## Hard rules

1. All `hooks` rules apply, especially source discipline, the single curiosity gap, the fold check, and the hard `anti-ai` gate. Where the two skills conflict, this one wins for milestone content.
2. The milestone never opens the post. Open with the workflow friction, the user reality, or the cost that existed before it. The product, round, hire, or number enters as the resolution.
3. Hype vocabulary is banned in openers: "thrilled to announce", "excited to share", "proud to", "big news", "game-changing", "revolutionary", and any superlative without a receipt ("fastest", "biggest", "first ever" with no verifiable backing).
4. Prefer exact numbers over vague scale words. "1,412 workspaces" beats "thousands of teams". "$4.2M" beats "a new round". When the source has no exact number, use the concrete reality instead of a vaguer number.
5. The hook must hold a reader who does not care about your company. It has to prove something about the market, the mistake, or the bet. If it only works for people already invested in you, it is an internal memo.
6. Never invent friction, costs, user behavior, or numbers the milestone does not actually have. A fabricated struggle is worse than a plain announcement.

## Procedure

1. Run the `hooks` procedure with the milestone material as the source. Before mining for voltage, name the milestone type: product update or launch, funding, hiring, or growth number.
2. Reconstruct the before-state. Interview the source for what existed before the milestone: what broke, what workaround people ran, what a week of the old way cost, what the team could not do. This before-state is where the hookable signals live, not in the news itself.
3. Apply the type guidance below to pick which part of the before-state carries the opener.
4. Draft openers with the milestone absent from the first line. Then run the stranger test: strip the company name and product name from the hook. If a reader with no stake in the company would not keep reading, discard it.
5. Place the milestone as the resolution beat, after the opener has earned it. Then run the standard `hooks` gates: fold check, emotion named, one open question, anti-ai scan.

## Milestone types

**Product update or launch: friction first.** Open with the specific pain the feature removes: the manual step, the workaround, the support ticket that kept recurring, the thing users did in a spreadsheet because the product could not do it. The sharper and more observable the friction, the harder the resolution lands. A feature list is not a before-state.

**Funding: the bet, not the amount.** A round is proof that a specific claim about the market convinced professional skeptics. Open with that claim, the industry mistake it corrects, or the thing that becomes possible now and was impossible last quarter. Use the exact amount when you use one at all, but the amount alone is a press release, not a hook.

**Hiring: the gap that made the role necessary.** Open with what the team could not do, the ball that kept getting dropped, or the moment the founder realized they were the bottleneck. The hire resolves the gap. A bio recital gives the reader nothing to feel.

**Growth milestone: the mechanism behind the number.** A round number is a stat; what produced it is a story. Open with the decision, channel, mistake, or reversal that bent the curve. If you cannot name the mechanism, the milestone is not ready to be a post.

## Examples

Bad: "Thrilled to announce our new bulk-edit feature is live!"
Better: "Our heaviest users were exporting to a spreadsheet, editing 200 rows, and importing them back. Some did it every Monday."
Why: the bad one asks the reader to celebrate; the better one opens the observable friction the feature resolves.

Bad: "We're excited to share that we've raised $12M to revolutionize freight!"
Better: "Every freight broker we interviewed kept a private spreadsheet their software vendor never saw. Last month, investors wrote us a $12M check because of that spreadsheet."
Why: the bad one reports the company's feelings; the better one names the market gap the round is a bet on, so a stranger keeps reading.

Bad: "We're proud to welcome our first VP of Sales to the team!"
Better: "I ran every sales call myself for two years. Last quarter I missed 14 demos because founder-led sales stopped scaling."
Why: the bad one is internal news; the better one names the gap the role exists to close, with a cost attached.

Bad: "We just crossed 10,000 users. So grateful for this incredible journey!"
Better: "9,000 of our first 10,000 users came through an integration we almost cut from the roadmap twice."
Why: the bad one shows a number and asks for applause; the better one opens the mechanism behind the number and leaves the near-miss unexplained.

## Self-check

- Is the milestone absent from the first line and present only as the resolution?
- Does the opener survive the stranger test with the company and product names removed?
- Is the opener free of hype vocabulary and unreceipted superlatives?
- Is every scale word an exact number where the source provides one?
- What does the hook prove about the market, the mistake, or the bet? Name it.
- Is the before-state real and sourced, not invented for drama?
- Did every finalist pass the full `hooks` self-check: emotion named, one open question, fold, anti-ai?
