# Newsjacking bake-off

Read this when you want to see the full delta the skill produces on one candidate story, or when calibrating the refusal boundary on a disqualified one.

Both trending stories below are SYNTHETIC: invented for this bake-off, published by no one, citing a research firm that does not exist. The founder material is real: Will Nelson, Founder of 64stories.com, quoted verbatim from his own published LinkedIn posts with his real numbers.

## Candidate A (synthetic story)

"New report: 70% of B2B buyers say they distrust AI-written content," published yesterday morning by an invented research firm. It is circulating hard in marketing LinkedIn and two trade newsletters have covered it.

Window check: about 20 hours old, window open.

### Baseline output (no skill)

> New study: 70% of B2B buyers say they distrust AI-written content.
>
> Let that sink in.
>
> Everyone is racing to automate their content. But buyers are telling us loud and clear: authenticity wins.
>
> AI is a tool, not a replacement for human connection. The brands that thrive will be the ones that keep the human touch.
>
> Do you trust AI-written content? Let me know in the comments.

What went wrong, line by line:

- The hook restates the headline. Every reader in the niche saw this stat yesterday; the first line spends the fold on old information.
- "Let that sink in" is filler theater, and a machine tell.
- The take is agreement. "Authenticity wins" is what the report already implies; this is an empty take, the exact thing rule 2 disqualifies.
- No experience, no number, no client moment. Any of the 10,000 people who saw the report could have posted this word for word. There is no standing on display.
- It closes with engagement bait, banned by the `linkedin-post` rules this skill inherits.

### With the skill

Rubric score (step 2): recency open at 20 hours. Relevance: sits inside Will's founder-led content pillar. Audience overlap: B2B founders and marketers are exactly who is sharing it. Standing: he runs founder content programs with published results. Angle: contrarian angle available, the report is being read as "AI bad, human good" and his data points elsewhere. All five pass.

Narrative-fit trace (step 3):

- Narrative line: content works when a company tells one true story and the founder tells it in his own voice; from his own post, "Your story is your strategy, made legible."
- Verbatim receipt: "In the last 90 days, 64stories has grown an established CEO's LinkedIn account by 500% by doing something really simple. We share company milestones through the CEO's voice."

Angle brief (step 5):

- Story: a widely shared report says 70% of B2B buyers distrust AI-written content.
- Earned angle: buyers are not detecting AI, they are detecting the absence of a real person and a true story; tooling touches our clients' posts and the posts still work, because a founder shapes each one and it stays true.
- Receipt: the 500% in 90 days CEO account, shaped together on a call every two weeks.

The post (drafted through `linkedin-post`, calibrated to his measured voice: median 79 words, one-line paragraphs, long reasoning sentences, contractions, no rhetorical questions, no hashtags, ends a beat early):

> The buyer trust survey making the rounds this week is getting read backwards.
>
> The replies are all victory laps about human writing beating AI writing.
>
> I don't think buyers are detecting AI. I think they're detecting absence.
>
> In the last 90 days, 64stories grew an established CEO's LinkedIn account by 500%, and software touched nearly every post. The part that made them work was human. Every two weeks we get on a call, shape the ideas together, and make sure each post is true and aligned to the story the company is telling.
>
> The distrust in that survey isn't about how the words got typed.
>
> It's about whether anyone is home.

Why this passes where the baseline failed:

- The hook references the story without repeating the stat, and takes a position the reader has not seen.
- The take disagrees with the consensus reading, so it survives the headline-deletion test: remove the survey and there is still a claim with a receipt under it.
- The proof is his real number from his real client work, quoted from his own published material, not invented for the moment.
- Voice matches his fingerprint: one-line paragraphs, an 18-word reasoning sentence in the middle, contractions, no questions, no exclamation points.
- It ends one beat early, on the sharpest line, with no summary and no bait.

Optional journalist note (step 7), for the trade newsletter writer covering the report, 52 words:

> Saw your piece on the buyer trust report. One data point from the other side: we grew an established CEO's LinkedIn account 500% in 90 days with software touching nearly every post, shaped with the founder on a biweekly call. Happy to walk you through the numbers today. Will Nelson, 64stories.com.

## Candidate B (synthetic story): the refusal

"Agency sector sheds 12,000 marketing jobs this quarter, trade tracker reports," invented for this bake-off, six hours old.

Rubric run: recency open. Relevance high, it is his industry. Audience overlap high. Standing exists. An angle is even available, the tempting "lean teams with AI win" post is sitting right there.

And none of that matters, because step 4 fires first:

> Declining this one. It is a layoff wave hurting real people, and the tragedy disqualifier refuses those without exception, regardless of relevance or how open the window is. No angle brief generated.

Why this is the correct output:

- Disqualifiers outrank the rubric. A candidate can score five for five and still be refused; the rubric decides whether a story is usable, the disqualifiers decide whether it is touchable.
- The refusal names the rule, so the user learns the boundary instead of just losing a post.
- The baseline pass, with no skill loaded, would have shipped "this proves what I have said for years about lean teams" over 12,000 people's worst week. That post costs more trust than any window is worth.
