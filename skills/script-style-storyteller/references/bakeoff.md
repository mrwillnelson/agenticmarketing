# Bake-off: chronology vs the tension cut

One real story seed, two 30 to 60 second scripts. The seed is grounded in Will Nelson's published LinkedIn post of 2026-03-03: a 64stories client hit 669,000 impressions in 28 days from founder content, the impressions kept climbing, and the mechanic, in Will's verbatim words, was "we're just capturing stories of the company and getting the founder to share them in his voice."

What is real: the 669,000 impressions, the 28 days, the still-climbing trajectory, and the mechanic quoted above. What is constructed: the founder's dialogue and the first-call scene below are invented for this exercise to demonstrate the beats, and are labeled where they appear. The client stays unnamed. In real use, hard rules 1 and 6 apply with no exceptions: every quote must be what was actually said, and any constructed connective beat gets flagged to the user before shipping.

## Baseline: the chronological retelling (about 55 seconds)

Every line below is true to the seed. That is the trap: it is all chronology and almost no story.

> A few months ago we signed a new client at 64stories, and I want to walk you through what happened.

Setup, not a cold open. This line could introduce any client story ever told, and it spends the entire 3-second fold saying "a story is coming" instead of starting one.

> First we ran our onboarding. We got to know the team, went through the founder's old content, and set up our Story Engineering process.

Three true events, zero tension. Nothing is at stake yet, and the first 8 to 10 second retention mark arrives during a process description. This is the second viewers leave.

> Then, every couple of weeks, we collected stories from around the company and turned them into LinkedIn posts in the founder's voice.

The actual mechanic is in here, but summarized. "Collected stories" describes; nothing is quoted, nothing is concrete, no beat raises a question the viewer needs answered.

> We kept publishing consistently, week after week, and over time the numbers started to improve.

The flattest sentence a story can contain. "Over time things improved" releases tension that was never raised, straight across another retention dip.

> After 28 days, the account had done 669,000 impressions, which was a great result for everyone involved.

The strongest fact in the whole seed arrives at second 45, delivered like a line item, to whoever is still watching. Almost nobody is.

> So the lesson here is that if you stay consistent and trust the process, founder-led content really does work.

The moral bow. It restates what the viewer already concluded, and it plays after the story is over.

## With the skill: the tension-cut version (about 35 seconds)

> "I don't have any stories." First call with a new client, that's the first thing the founder tells me.

Cold open mid-action, and it is dialogue, not description. The line drops the viewer inside a specific moment and opens the exact loop the video will close. This quote is constructed for the exercise; in real use it must be the sentence that was actually said, or the open gets built from one that was.

> And everything we charge for rests on one claim. The stories are already there.

Tension beat, one concrete detail: the claim itself. The stakes are now personal to the narrator, and the founder's line and this claim cannot both be right. Constructed beat, labeled as such.

> So we didn't write anything new. We sat in on his calls and wrote down what he was already saying.

The turn, told as the decision made at the time. This is the real mechanic from Will's post, "capturing stories of the company and getting the founder to share them in his voice", compressed into what-we-did words rather than hindsight language.

> His words. His voice. Our job was catching them.

Release beat and pattern break: three short fragments after longer lines, right on the retention mark. It pays off the cold open directly: the stories existed, they were just spoken instead of written.

> Day 28, that account had done 669,000 impressions. Still climbing.

The landing. Both numbers are real. What changed is stated plainly, and the script stops a beat early: no line about acquisition cost, no lesson about consistency, no invitation to trust the process. The viewer draws the conclusion the baseline insisted on spelling out.

## The beat ledger

The tension cut, applied to every beat the chronological version contained:

- "We signed a new client": neither raises nor releases. Cut; the cold open implies it.
- Onboarding and getting to know the team: neither. Cut.
- Reviewing old content: neither. Cut.
- Setting up Story Engineering: neither in this story. Cut; it is a different video.
- "I don't have any stories" (constructed): raises. Kept, promoted to cold open.
- Everything rests on one claim (constructed): raises. Kept.
- Sat in on calls, wrote down what he said: releases into the turn. Kept.
- "Publishing consistently, week after week": neither. Cut.
- "Numbers started to improve": releases nothing that was raised. Cut.
- 669,000 impressions in 28 days, still climbing: releases everything. Kept, as the landing.
- The lesson about consistency: neither, and after the ending. Cut.

Eleven beats became five. Nothing that survived is filler, and nothing that was cut is missed.

## What changed, in five lines

- About 150 spoken words became about 80, and the shorter script contains both real numbers plus a quoted line; the longer one had one number and no quotes.
- The open moved from "a story is coming" to inside the story's most tense instant, which happened to sit in the middle of the chronology.
- Summary became dialogue at the two moments where words carried the tension.
- The turn moved from hindsight ("the numbers started to improve") to the decision as made at the time.
- The moral bow became a hard cut on the result, one beat before the viewer expected the ending.
