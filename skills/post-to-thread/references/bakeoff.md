# Bake-off: chop-at-280 vs beat recut

Read this when you want to see what the skill changes on a real post. Same source, two threads: the baseline splits the post at 280 characters, the way a cross-posting tool does it; the with-skill version extracts the beat structure and recuts each beat for X.

Every failure mode in one artifact. Tweet 1 crams three beats plus the opening clause of a fourth, so the one tweet most readers will ever see is a wall with no reason to expand. Tweet 2 ends mid-sentence on "When you try to explain your strategy"; tweet 3 opens with the orphaned half. Tweets 3 and 4 both break mid-thought. Tweet 5 ends the thread on "Read the full piece:" with a dangling colon and no argument. And because the text is identical to the LinkedIn post, publishing both in one day violates the same-day law by construction.

## With the skill

Step trace.

1. Beat structure, named before touching a word:
   - Hook: a deflection presented as a diagnosis. Founders route the storytelling question to marketing; the routing is the tell.
   - Turn 1, the reframe: can't explain the company means no clear strategy. Two problems collapse into one.
   - Turn 2, the pattern: seen dozens of times; the "too technical" excuse hides undecided identity.
   - Turn 3, the mechanism: storytelling forces clarity; narrative gaps expose strategy gaps.
   - Turn 4, the scale consequence: sales and product tell different stories; the cost lands in roadmaps and customers.
   - Turn 5, the resolution: scaling founders become the chief storyteller and repeat the story until it comes back.
   - Landing: "Your story is your strategy, made legible."
   - Trailing matter: "Read the full piece:" is distribution, not a beat. It does not travel; a link costs reach on X, and the thread carries the argument itself.
2. Tweet 1 recut for the hook law, tested as a standalone post.
3. One beat per tweet, each compressed by translation.
4. Re-landed: the landing line gets the final tweet, alone. No link tweet, no CTA; Will's measured fingerprint ends a beat early, so the thread does too.

The recut thread, annotated:

> Ask a founder about storytelling. They point you to marketing.
>
> That answer is the whole diagnosis.

Tweet 1, the hook. Same deflection-as-diagnosis move, recut from 17 words to 16 so the verdict sits on its own line. Stands alone: no thread announcement, no LinkedIn pointer, and it reads complete if nobody taps.

> If you can't clearly explain what your company does, you don't have a clear strategy.
>
> Not two problems. One.

Turn 1. The reframe keeps its full sentence because the sentence is the claim. "The two problems are the same problem" recuts to four words that hit harder at X pace.

> I've seen this dozens of times. A founder struggles to tell their story and blames it on being "too technical."
>
> Dig deeper: they haven't decided what they are and what they're not.

Turn 2. The verbatim quote "too technical" survives untouched, per the verbatim law. "Made the hard decisions about" compresses to "decided"; the beat loses connective tissue, not content.

> Storytelling forces clarity.
>
> Explain your strategy to a stranger and you find out what you don't understand.
>
> The gaps in the narrative are the gaps in the strategy.

Turn 3, the mechanism. The 21-word LinkedIn sentence ("When you try to explain your strategy to someone who knows nothing about your space, you discover what you don't understand.") becomes a 14-word tweet line. Translation, not summary: the move is intact, the setup clause is gone.

> This compounds as you scale.
>
> Your head of sales tells one story. Your head of product tells another.
>
> Not a positioning problem. It surfaces as misaligned roadmaps and confused customers.

Turn 4. The two-stories image stays concrete because it is the proof. The consequence list trims to the two costs a reader can picture; "dozens of times" already carried the frequency two beats ago.

> The founders who scale become the chief storyteller.
>
> They pick the story, then tell it until everyone can repeat it back.

Turn 5. "Figure out what story they're telling" recuts to "pick the story"; the repeat-it-back image stays because it is the beat.

> Your story is your strategy, made legible.

The landing, verbatim. The one line that travels intact, because no recut beats it and the identical-text law bans duplicated posts, not a quoted landing line. On LinkedIn this line was followed by "Read the full piece:"; on X it is followed by nothing. The re-land is the deletion.

## Why the recut beats the chop

1. Tweet 1 is a post, not a fragment pile. The chop's tweet 1 spends its 272 characters on three and a half beats; the recut's spends 99 on one move with a payoff line. The reader who sees only tweet 1, which is most readers, gets a complete provocation instead of a truncated paragraph.
2. Every recut tweet survives a screenshot. The chop produces "When you try to explain your strategy 2/5" and "Positioning isn't 3/5" as tweet endings; quoted alone, either one embarrasses the argument. Each recut tweet is a claim plus its support.
3. Compression kept the specifics. "Dozens of times", the "too technical" quote, the sales-versus-product split, and the landing line all made the crossing; only connective tissue died. The chop technically keeps everything and therefore emphasizes nothing.
4. The arc is the post's arc, re-paced. Seven tweets, one per beat, in the source order: diagnosis, reframe, pattern, mechanism, cost, resolution, landing. The chop has no arc, only arithmetic.
5. The ending lands instead of trailing off. The chop ends on a dangling "Read the full piece:" with no link. The recut ends on the post's strongest sentence and stops, and the article link, if Will wants it on X, becomes its own post where the link is the point.
6. The thread is publishable alongside the post. The chop is the identical text, so shipping both same-day is a repost. The recut shares one quoted landing line and can sit a day apart from the LinkedIn original as a native second performance.
