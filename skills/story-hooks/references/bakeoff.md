# Bake-off: setup-first opener vs mid-action opener

Read this when you want to see what the skill changes on one real-shaped story idea. Same source, two openers: the baseline starts where the story started, the with-skill version starts where it hurt.

## The story idea

Will Nelson, Founder of 64stories.com, is real, and so are the numbers below: 64stories grew an established CEO's LinkedIn account by 500% in 90 days by sharing company milestones in the CEO's voice, through a biweekly call where Will proposes post ideas and the two shape them together. Those facts come from Will's published posts. The confession framing wrapped around them, Will's initial doubt and his call notes, is constructed for demonstration, in the way a story like this would arrive from a debrief call.

The idea, as it lands in the idea bank:

> On the first call of the engagement, Will pushed back on plain milestone posts and argued for cleverer angles. His notes from that meeting mark the milestone idea as too plain to work. The client went with milestones in his own voice anyway. Ninety days later the account had grown 500%, and the notes were still in the doc.

Shape: confession. Cost test, stated plainly before drafting: "I argued against the strategy that worked, and the client was right." It flatters nobody. Floor check: a professional misjudgment, fully resolved, client unnamed. It clears the floor, so no need to ask the user first.

## Baseline: without the skill

A setup-first opener, starting where the story started:

> A few months ago, 64stories began working with an established CEO on his LinkedIn presence. Over the following 90 days, we grew his account by 500%. But before I explain how we did it, I want to rewind to our very first call, because I almost talked the client out of the exact strategy that ended up working. There's a lesson in there about trusting simple ideas.

Everything a reader needs to stop reading is present: a date range, a company introduction, a promised rewind, and the lesson pre-announced before the story earns it. The confession is flagged ("I almost talked the client out of") but arrives buried in sentence three, below the LinkedIn fold.

## With the skill

Step trace, kept short:

1. Shape named: confession. The cost sentence exists and flatters no one, so it survives the cost test.
2. Peak found: not the kickoff call (the beginning) but the moment the 90-day number landed next to the old notes. The post opens there; the first call becomes body material.
3. Artifact hunted: the call notes with "too plain to work" written next to the milestone idea. One artifact, small, and it was actually in the doc.
4. Lenses: Oh shit leads (watching your own confident judgment be wrong in writing), Me too close behind (everyone has argued hardest against the thing that worked).
5. Tense and person: past tense, I throughout. The result belongs to "we"; the misjudgment does not.

The two finalists:

> I spent our first call trying to talk a CEO out of the strategy that grew his account 500% in 90 days.

Emotion: Oh shit. Open question: what was the strategy, and why was Will wrong about it. Fold: 103 characters, whole line visible on LinkedIn.

> My notes from that first call say "too plain to work" next to the idea that did all of it.

Emotion: Me too. Open question: what was the idea. Fold: 90 characters. The artifact carries the confession; no feeling is named anywhere.

## Why the mid-action opener wins

1. The reader arrives inside the moment. The baseline spends 40 words reaching the point where the with-skill version begins. "A few months ago, 64stories began working with" is setup nobody was there for.
2. The confession leads instead of hiding. In the baseline, the admission sits in sentence three; above the fold the post reads as an agency brag. In the rewrite, the first clause is the misjudgment.
3. The artifact replaces the adjective. The baseline gestures at a lesson about "trusting simple ideas." The notes that say "too plain to work" prove the same thing and leave a question open.
4. One gap, not three promises. The baseline promises a how, a rewind, and a lesson. Each finalist leaves exactly one question, which is the parent skill's law working.
5. The cost stays owned. "I spent our first call trying to talk a CEO out of" keeps the confession in first person; the baseline's "we grew his account" then "I almost" mixes credit and blame in the same breath.
6. It matches the voice. Both finalists run past tense, contraction-light, no exclamation, no rhetorical question, and sit far under Will's measured 210-character LinkedIn fold with room for a second beat.
