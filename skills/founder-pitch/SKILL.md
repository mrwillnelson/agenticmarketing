# Founder Pitch

Earned media for founders is not a press release blasted to a list. It is a specific story, matched to a specific outlet, written for that outlet's audience. The founder-first reality: podcasts and niche newsletters say yes far more often than tier-1 tech press, and their audiences convert better because they chose to be there. Weight the list accordingly. A 5,000-subscriber newsletter your ICP actually reads beats a mention nobody in your market sees.

## Hard rules

1. Never pitch a story you would not post about yourself. If it is not strong enough for your own feed, it is not strong enough for someone else's audience.
2. Never fake familiarity with someone's work. Reference a specific piece only if you actually read or listened to it. One fabricated compliment ends the relationship before it starts.
3. Consent before naming anyone. A customer, partner, or investor appears in a pitch only after they have agreed, in writing, to talk on the record.
4. Exclusives are real. One outlet at a time, and the embargo holds even when a better outlet replies late. Breaking one exclusive burns every future one.
5. The pitch serves their audience, the coverage serves yours, in that order. If you cannot say what their readers or listeners get, do not send it.
6. Under 150 words, one clear yes-or-no ask, no attachments on the first touch.
7. Hype vocabulary is banned: revolutionary, disruptive, game-changing, cutting-edge, synergy, and their cousins. Every pitch passes the `anti-ai` skill's scan before it goes out.
8. Exactly one follow-up, 5 to 7 days later, carrying one new piece of information. After that, silence is the answer, same as `engagement-to-pipeline`'s rule.

## Procedure

1. Build the target list, 20 to 40 names. Work backwards from where your ICP already is: the watering holes in `my-story/icp.md`, plus whoever covered companies adjacent to yours. Three columns: podcasts your ICP listens to, newsletters they read, and the handful of journalists who genuinely cover your space. Podcasts and newsletters should dominate the list; tier-1 press is a lottery ticket, not a plan.
2. Score each target on four dimensions, high, medium, or low:
   - Audience-ICP overlap: would your buyer actually see this?
   - Beat fit: do they cover your topic? Read their last 5 pieces or episode descriptions before deciding. A guess here produces rule-2 violations later.
   - Accessibility: a 5,000-subscriber niche newsletter answers email; a tier-1 reporter gets 300 pitches a day. Score honestly.
   - Timing: are they actively covering your theme right now, this month?
   Pitch highs first. A medium on overlap with a high on accessibility usually beats the reverse.
3. Pick the angle. Six founder-first templates:
   - Data story: one real number from your business or content performance, plus what it implies for their audience.
   - Milestone exclusive: an announcement without hype, framed with `milestone-hooks` discipline, offered to one outlet with a clear embargo date.
   - Op-ed or guest essay: a provocative thesis from your pillars, three supporting points, and the credential that earns you the byline.
   - Customer story: a named customer who has already agreed, on the record, to tell their story; secure that consent before the pitch is written.
   - Trend connector: three concrete signals you observed firsthand, plus the pattern they form. Firsthand means you saw it, not that you read about it.
   - Newsjack response: when the angle is a reaction to breaking news, hand off to the `newsjacking` skill; that is the fast lane and it has its own clock.
4. Write the pitch, under 150 words:
   - The subject line does the work: a specific stat or a specific thesis. Never "quick question", never "partnership opportunity".
   - The first line proves you know their work: reference a specific recent piece or episode, honestly, per rule 2.
   - The ask is one clear yes-or-no question. Not three options, not "thoughts?".
   - No attachments. Link your press kit in the signature instead; build it once with [references/press-kit.md](references/press-kit.md).
   - Run the draft through the `anti-ai` scan. Rule 7 vocabulary is an automatic rewrite.
5. Follow up once, 5 to 7 days later, adding one new piece of information: a fresh number, a new signal, a published post that proves the angle. Then stop. Log non-responders as quiet and keep them on the list for a genuinely new story later.
6. Log outcomes. Append a dated block to `my-story/what-worked.md`: which angles got replies, which target types said yes, which subject lines got opened into responses. The second campaign should be built on the first one's data.

To see the whole motion against one target, read [references/bakeoff.md](references/bakeoff.md): a 280-word baseline pitch versus the with-skill version, annotated line by line.

## Personalization

Reads from `my-story/`:

- `icp.md`: the watering holes drive step 1, and audience-ICP overlap in step 2 is scored against it. If empty, ask the user where their last three customers spend attention before building any list; a target list without an ICP is a spam list.
- `pillars.md`: op-ed theses and trend-connector patterns come from here, so the coverage compounds the same argument the content makes.
- `narrative.md`: the filter for which stories are worth pitching at all. An angle that is off-arc earns coverage that does not accumulate.
- `voice-model.md`: the pitch is written in the founder's voice, not press-release voice. A pitch that sounds like an agency gets treated like one.

With an empty pack the skill still runs the procedure and enforces every hard rule, but targeting blocks on the ICP questions above and pitches come back marked "generic voice, review closely".

## Examples

Bad subject line: "Quick question about a partnership opportunity"
Better: "669k impressions in 28 days from one founder's LinkedIn"
Why: the bad one is the most deleted subject line in media; the better one is a specific number that is itself the story.

Bad first line: "Longtime fan of your newsletter, love everything you write!"
Better: "Your issue on founder content beating brand accounts argued the gap is trust. I have a number that supports it and one that complicates it."
Why: the bad one is unfalsifiable flattery every recipient has learned to distrust; the better one proves the sender read a specific piece and engages its actual argument.

Bad ask: "Would love to explore a feature, a podcast swap, or maybe an intro call to see where there's synergy?"
Better: "Would a short breakdown of that 28-day run work for a future issue? Yes or no is a fine answer."
Why: three vague asks means the recipient has to do the thinking; one yes-or-no question takes ten seconds to answer, and banned vocabulary is banned.

Bad follow-up: "Just floating this back to the top of your inbox!"
Better: "One update since I wrote: the same account crossed 1M impressions this week, so the 28-day number was not a spike. Still a fit?"
Why: a bump asks them to reread old mail; new information gives them a fresh reason to say yes, and it is the only follow-up they will get.

## Self-check

- Would I post this story on my own feed this week? If no, stop.
- Did I actually read or listen to the piece the first line references?
- Is every named person consented, in writing, before the send?
- If this is an exclusive, is it with exactly one outlet, with a written embargo date?
- Is the pitch under 150 words with one yes-or-no ask and no attachments?
- Does the subject line carry a specific stat or thesis, and zero rule-7 vocabulary?
- Can I state in one sentence what their audience gets from this story?
- Is the follow-up plan exactly one message with one new fact, then silence?
- Is there a dated outcomes block ready to land in `my-story/what-worked.md`?
