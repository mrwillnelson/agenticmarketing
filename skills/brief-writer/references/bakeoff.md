# Bake-off: the lazy brief versus the full brief

One real post, two briefs. The post is by Will Nelson, Founder of 64stories.com, published on LinkedIn on 2026-02-26: 141 reactions, 35 comments, 195 words, the top performer in his measured set. Below it: the one-paragraph brief most people would type, then the brief this skill produces, reconstructed from the finished post. The reconstruction is labeled as such: it was reverse-engineered after publication to show what a complete assignment for this post looks like, not recovered from an archive.

## The post (verbatim)

> Ask a founder about storytelling and they'll point you to their marketing team.
>
> That tells you everything.
>
> If you can't clearly explain what your company does, you probably don't have a clear strategy. The two problems are the same problem.
>
> I've seen this dozens of times. A founder struggles to tell their story. They blame it on being "too technical." But dig deeper and the real issue surfaces. They haven't made the hard decisions about what they are and what they're not.
>
> Storytelling forces clarity. When you try to explain your strategy to someone who knows nothing about your space, you discover what you don't understand. The gaps in your narrative reveal the gaps in your strategy.
>
> This matters more as you scale. Your head of sales tells one story, your head of product tells another. Positioning isn't the problem. That's a strategy problem that will show up as misaligned roadmaps, confused customers, and teams working toward different goals.
>
> The founders who scale become the chief storyteller. They figure out what story they're telling, then tell it repeatedly until everyone can repeat it back.
>
> Your story is your strategy, made legible.

## The lazy baseline brief

> Write a LinkedIn post about how storytelling and strategy are connected for founders. Make the point that if you can't explain your company clearly you probably don't have a clear strategy. Keep it punchy and authentic, in Will's voice. Maybe end with a strong one-liner.

This is what "prompt-typing" looks like. It contains a real insight, and it would still produce a generic post: no named reader, no fear, no whitelist of usable material, no shape, no trap list. The drafter fills every gap with the most statistically likely choice, which is exactly what the voice model exists to prevent.

## The full brief (reconstruction)

# Brief: Storytelling is strategy, made legible

- Thesis: Your inability to explain your company is not a communication weakness; it is your unmade strategy decisions showing through.
- Audience: Technical founders past product-market fit who have delegated "messaging" to marketing, believe explaining is a skill they personally lack, and are starting to see their leaders describe the company differently.
- Reader tension: The founder who blames his story problems on being "too technical" and quietly fears that the real issue is he has never decided what the company is and is not.
- Hook direction:
  - Angle A: the deflection as the tell. Open on what founders do when asked about storytelling (point at marketing), and let that gesture indict itself. Emotion: recognition.
  - Angle B: the reframe as accusation. Open directly on the two-problems-are-one-problem claim. Emotion: dread.
  - Angle C: the scale consequence. Open on five leaders telling five stories. Emotion: dread. (Held for a follow-up; used later in the 2026-03-04 post.)
- Source truth:
  - Observation (author's own client work, repeated pattern): founders who struggle to tell their story blame being "too technical"; deeper digging shows unmade decisions about what they are and are not. Claim strength: "I've seen this dozens of times", no client named, no number invented.
  - Fact (author's own experience): as companies scale, sales and product leaders tell different stories, surfacing as misaligned roadmaps and confused customers.
  - No transcript excerpts exist for this piece. All material is the author's first-hand pattern observation, and the draft must present it that way: "I've seen", not fabricated case specifics.
- Shape: argument piece, 5 beats. Open on the deflection. Indict it in one line. State the reframe (same problem). Prove with the observed pattern, then the scale consequence. Land on a compressed restatement of the thesis. Structure family: argument.
- Platform and length: LinkedIn. 150 to 220 words: an argument needs room for reasoning; upper-middle of the measured range (median 79, long tail genuinely used).
- Voice rules for this piece: sentences long enough to carry reasoning (measured median 18 words); one-line paragraphs; no rhetorical questions even though the reframe invites them; no exclamation points; end a beat after the strongest line, not with a summary.
- Avoid for this piece: do not open by defining storytelling; do not cite the usual famous-brand narrative examples (this piece's proof is the author's pattern, not borrowed logos); do not resolve with "hire better marketing" or "run a messaging workshop", because the thesis is that messaging is not the problem; do not invent a client anecdote to make "dozens of times" concrete.
- CTA decision: one plain pointer to the full essay, nothing performing. No engagement bait; the post must stand alone if nobody clicks.
- Success signal: comments from founders describing their own version of the pattern (their leaders telling different stories), not generic agreement.

## Why the full brief changes the draft

- The reader tension names the person and the fear, and the finished post spends a full paragraph on exactly that person: the founder who blames "too technical". The lazy brief's drafter has no idea whose chest the first line should land in, so it lands in no one's.
- Source truth is honest about being thin. It declares there are no transcripts or client numbers, so the draft says "I've seen this dozens of times" and stops there. The lazy brief's drafter, needing proof, invents a composite client story, and `draft-qa`'s proof trace kills the draft a step later. The brief prevented the fabrication instead of catching it.
- Hook direction gave three angles and the reasoning to pick one. Angle A (the deflection) beat angle B (the bare claim) because recognition outperforms accusation as an opener; the bare claim still appears, but as line three, after the reader is implicated. The lazy brief would almost certainly have produced angle B as the opener, because the thesis sentence is the most copyable thing in it.
- The avoid-list is piece-specific and each item is visible in the final post as an absence: no storytelling definition, no borrowed famous-brand examples, no workshop advice, no invented anecdote. A global ban list ("no hashtags, no em dashes") would have prevented none of these, because none of them are globally banned; they are only wrong here.
- The voice rules picked the 3 to 5 rules this shape endangers. An argument piece tempts polish and rhetorical questions; the post has neither, and its ending ("Your story is your strategy, made legible.") stops a beat early instead of summarizing, exactly as the piece rule demanded.
- The score did its quiet work too: source truth at 70 is a warning label the drafter can read. The same brief with a fabricated quote would score higher and be worse. Honest scoring is what lets the 70 threshold mean anything.
